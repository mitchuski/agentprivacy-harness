# Field Guide: Preparing a Home for a 72-Hour Power Outage

Prepare an ordinary household to manage safely and comfortably through a power outage lasting up to seventy-two hours. Assumes a temperate climate and no backup generator. Work the sections in order; the earlier ones matter most.

## Water
- Store four litres per person per day = twelve litres per person for the full seventy-two hours; half for drinking, half for cooking and basic hygiene.
- Use food-grade containers kept out of direct sunlight; replace stored water every six months.
- If a storm is forecast and you have advance warning, also fill the bathtub — that water is for flushing toilets and washing, never for drinking.

## Food
- Keep a dedicated shelf of non-perishable, no-cook food for three days: canned beans, canned fish, peanut butter, crackers, dried fruit, shelf-stable milk.
- Own a manual can opener, because an electric one will be useless.
- Eat in order: perishable food from the refrigerator first, then the freezer, and only then the emergency shelf.

## Refrigerator and freezer
- Safe holding times: an unopened refrigerator about four hours; a full freezer about forty-eight hours; a half-full freezer about twenty-four hours.
- Keep both doors closed as much as possible and tape a reminder note to each door.
- Keep the freezer consolidated and full, even if some space is containers of frozen water.
- Discard any perishable food that has spent more than two hours above four degrees Celsius; when in doubt, throw it out.

## Light
- Primary source: battery-powered LED lanterns or headlamps. Avoid candles entirely, since they are a leading cause of house fires during outages.
- Store at least one light per person plus two spare sets of batteries for each light.
- Keep one headlamp beside each bed and one lantern in the kitchen; place them where they can be found in the dark.

## Heat and safety
- Never run a camping stove, barbecue, or generator indoors, in a garage, or near a window: each produces carbon monoxide, which is odourless and can be fatal.
- Install at least one battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm on each floor where people sleep.
- In cold weather, choose one small room on a south-facing side of the home, close its door, and gather everyone there; layered clothing and blankets are safer than any improvised heater.
- If the indoor temperature falls below ten degrees Celsius and you cannot maintain warmth, relocate to a warming centre or a neighbour's home.

## Communication and information
- Keep a battery-powered or hand-crank radio for emergency broadcasts, and know your local emergency station's frequency before the outage begins.
- When a storm is forecast, charge every phone and power bank; a full modern power bank of twenty thousand milliamp-hours will recharge a typical phone roughly four times.
- At the start of the outage, put your phone in low-power mode.
- Agree in advance on one out-of-area contact whom every member of the household will text, because texts get through congested networks more reliably than voice calls.

## Medical needs
- Keep a first-aid kit and a seven-day supply of every prescription medication, and rotate them so they stay in date.
- Refrigerated medication such as insulin generally remains usable at room temperature for up to twenty-eight days, but confirm the rule for your specific medication with a pharmacist before an emergency.
- Write the phone numbers of your pharmacy, your doctor, and the poison-control line on paper, because a dead phone is not a phone book.

## When the power returns
- Wait five minutes before switching major appliances back on, so the grid can stabilise.
- Check the freezer: if food still contains ice crystals, it can be safely refrozen.
- Restock everything used during the outage within one week, starting with water and batteries.
- Write a one-page note about what was missing or awkward, and fix at least one of those gaps before the next outage arrives.
